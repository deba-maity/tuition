The telescope arrived in a long padded case, accompanied by instructions that seemed more complicated than the instrument. The astronomy club had borrowed it for one evening. Its members wanted photographs of the moon to display at assembly, and the clearest image would, they imagined, be the measure of a successful event. Cloud covered the sky before sunset. While the pupils waited, their guest, an amateur observer named Ms Sen, asked them to describe what they expected to see. Several spoke confidently of colours they had seen in space photographs. She explained that published images could combine long exposures, filters and processing. Looking through an eyepiece was a different experience, not necessarily a poorer one. When the clouds thinned, the first view lasted only a few seconds. A pupil called it disappointing. The moon looked small, and the telescope shook whenever somebody leaned against the table. Ms Sen lowered the magnification, moved the instrument to firmer ground and asked each viewer to find one feature near the boundary between light and shadow. At that boundary, low sunlight made the relief easier to distinguish. The pupils started comparing observations. One described a dark oval; another noticed a bright edge beside it. They made quick sketches in a notebook, not for display but to check whether they had seen the same feature. A temporary clearing gave them a second chance to look. This time nobody hurried to photograph the whole view. They knew what they wanted to examine. Their assembly presentation contained one imperfect photograph, several observations and an explanation of why the first attempt had failed. The telescope had not brought distant objects conveniently close. It had shown how movement, expectation and the choice of a question affect seeing. The club returned it the next morning with the instructions carefully folded. They now regarded the least glamorous pages as part of the instrument.
