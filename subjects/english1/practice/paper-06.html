The proposed path across the hillside looked excellent on the planner’s drawing. It was shorter than the winding track people already used and would reduce the walk to the health centre. The village committee welcomed it. Only the guide who led school walks asked why the existing track turned so far to the east before climbing. At the planning meeting his question seemed like resistance to improvement. The old route was inconvenient, and everybody knew it. Rather than repeat the objection, he invited two committee members to walk the direct line after rain. Within minutes their shoes were sinking into soft ground. A small channel, invisible in dry weather, had become a stream. Higher up, loose stones crossed the slope where water had washed away the soil. The guide did not claim that the older path was perfect. It was narrow in places and difficult for an injured person. He explained that it followed firmer ground, approached a crossing at a shallower angle and avoided the steepest patch. Its apparent detour preserved information that the drawing had not included. The committee asked the planner to walk both routes before revising the scheme. The final proposal widened part of the existing track, added a handrail at one steep section and made a short new connection to the centre. It saved less distance than the first design, but needed fewer retaining works and was safer in wet weather. A temporary marker was placed where the new section began so that users could report difficulties before permanent surfacing. At the opening there was no claim that local knowledge had defeated expert planning. The planner’s measurements had made several improvements possible. The guide’s experience had revealed the conditions those measurements needed to address. Both were necessary. What changed was the committee’s willingness to treat a troublesome question as information rather than disloyalty.
