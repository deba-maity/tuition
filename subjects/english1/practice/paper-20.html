4 [20]
A bench stood under the only large tree in the station forecourt. When a café sought permission to put tables there, the proposal described the area as unused space. The station manager almost signed the temporary licence. Then the cleaner asked whether the word “unused” meant nobody had paid for it or nobody had sat there. For three days she kept a simple count during her breaks. She saw delivery workers waiting between jobs, a father feeding a child, passengers eating food brought from home and an elderly man who seemed to come only to watch the trains. None stayed long enough to attract attention. Their separate pauses did not look like an organised use, but together they occupied most of the day. The manager asked the café owner to discuss a different arrangement. The owner pointed out that a business paid rent and employed people; a bench produced no direct income. The cleaner agreed, but asked what would happen to somebody who could not afford a drink. The question was not whether the café deserved space. It was whether every useful place had to require a purchase. They measured the forecourt together. By moving a storage bin and narrowing an unnecessarily wide advertising stand, they found room for two café tables without removing the public bench or blocking the path used by wheelchair passengers. The licence specified the table positions and kept the bench outside the café’s customer area. The elderly man returned the next week. He looked at the new tables, sat down and opened a paper parcel. Nobody asked him to order anything. The cleaner’s count had not measured comfort, dignity or the relief of resting without explanation. It had simply made an ordinary use visible long enough for those deciding about the space to acknowledge it.
