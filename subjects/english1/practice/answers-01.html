Option 1 [20]
Write a story beginning: “The envelope had been lying on the classroom floor all morning.” Develop a convincing conflict and an ending that follows from the events. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

The envelope had been lying on the classroom floor all morning. I noticed it only when my chair caught its corner. Inside were six hundred rupees and a folded note reading, “For the journey.” There was no name.

For a moment I thought of the science excursion. My own permission form was still unsigned because the bus fare was more than my mother could spare that week. The money would have solved an awkward conversation. I put the envelope on my desk instead.

At lunch I asked the class monitor whether anyone had reported a loss. She shook her head. Two boys immediately offered to claim it, laughing until I asked them what was written on the note. Neither knew. I decided that another general announcement would only attract more guesses.

Our teacher took the envelope to the office and asked me to come back after the last lesson. By then I regretted handing it over. Someone had probably been careless with money they hardly needed, I told myself, while my own form lay useless in my bag.

When I returned, the office assistant was standing beside a younger pupil. The boy’s eyes were red. His grandmother had given him the money for a bus journey to visit his father in hospital. He described the note before the teacher showed it to him. Then he held the envelope with both hands, as carefully as if it might break.

Outside, he thanked me and hurried towards the gate. I watched him go, embarrassed by the story I had invented about the unknown owner.

That evening I told my mother about the excursion and the envelope. We still had to work out the fare; honesty had not magically paid it. But the conversation was easier than I had feared. For once I had brought home a problem without bringing home an excuse.

Option 2 [20]
Write an original story ending: “For the first time, I was glad that things had not gone according to plan.” Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

I had rehearsed my introduction to the science exhibition until I could deliver it without looking at my notes. Every label was straight, every switch worked, and our model was ready before the judges arrived. Then the hall lights failed.

For a few seconds nobody moved. Our electric pump stopped, leaving the demonstration channel dry. I pressed the switch twice, as though determination might supply the missing current. Across the aisle, someone laughed nervously. My partner, Farah, began gathering the visitors around our table.

“We can show you the principle without the motor,” she said.

That had not been part of my introduction. I whispered that we should wait for the electricity. Farah pointed to the clear container above the channel and asked me to hold it higher. When she opened the small outlet, water ran through the model under gravity. Our carefully painted landscape was suddenly doing its work without the equipment on which I had depended.

A judge asked why raising the container increased the flow. I started the sentence I had memorised, realised it did not answer him, and stopped. Farah described the height difference. I added an explanation about the available gravitational potential energy. For the first time that morning, we were discussing the model instead of performing beside it.

Younger pupils asked whether the same arrangement would work in their gardens. We tried different heights, collected the water and repeated the demonstration. One attempt overflowed the channel, giving us an unexpected opportunity to explain the model’s limitations. I crossed out two impressive but empty phrases in my notebook.

The lights returned before the judging ended. We used the pump again, but this time we explained what it changed and what it did not. We did not win the largest prize. We did, however, answer every question ourselves. Packing away the dripping containers, I looked at my unused opening speech and smiled.

For the first time, I was glad that things had not gone according to plan.

Option 3 [20]
Describe a place that changes noticeably between early morning and late evening. Convey the contrast through details and your response to it. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

At six in the morning the railway forecourt belonged to people who knew where they were going. Porters pushed empty trolleys across the paving, their wheels rattling in the cool air. A tea seller lifted his first kettle, and the steam briefly hid his face.

I waited on the public bench while my father collected a ticket. Without the evening crowds, the square looked unexpectedly large. A cleaner moved fallen leaves into a neat line. Two commuters stood apart beneath the timetable, already dressed for offices that would not open for hours. Their silence was purposeful rather than uncomfortable.

The shops were closed, but the station was awake. Announcements travelled clearly across the open space. Each arrival produced a short burst of movement, followed by another quiet interval. I could distinguish individual sounds: a suitcase closing, coins touching a counter, a bicycle bell beyond the gate.

When I returned that evening, the bench was almost hidden. People waited beside bags, children circled a pillar, and a driver called a destination until it became part of the general noise. The tea seller no longer needed to raise his voice; customers had formed a patient queue beside his stove.

Light changed the forecourt more completely than the crowd did. Shop signs shone against the darkening sky. Headlamps swept across faces, revealing an expression for a second before moving on. The pale paving that had seemed spacious in the morning became a patchwork of shadows and scattered wrappers.

Yet the same cleaner was there, collecting the day’s remains. I recognised his careful movement before I recognised his face. The square’s two lives suddenly seemed connected by work that continued through both.

In the morning I had thought of the forecourt as a place of departures. By evening it looked equally like a place of reunions and delay. Neither impression was false. The place had not changed its purpose; a full day had simply shown me more of the people who depended on it.

Option 4 [20]
“Competition brings out the best in students.” Present a reasoned argument for or against the statement, considering a serious opposing view. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

Competition can bring out excellent work, but it does not automatically bring out the best in every student. Its value depends on what is rewarded, how fairly it is organised and what happens to those who do not win.

A clear goal can make practice purposeful. A debate scheduled for a particular day gives speakers a reason to test their arguments rather than postpone preparation. A race allows a runner to compare an actual performance with previous times. When competitors respect one another, each can discover possibilities that comfortable practice alone might not reveal.

Competition can also teach an important discipline: accepting an outcome without making it an excuse. A student who loses and then studies the judge’s comments may learn more than a winner who stops improving. The result is useful when it begins a conversation about performance rather than ending one about personal worth.

However, a school that celebrates only first place creates avoidable damage. Students may choose easy events, conceal mistakes or refuse to help classmates whom they see only as rivals. Unequal access to coaching and equipment can make a supposedly fair contest measure opportunity as much as effort. Repeated defeat without guidance can discourage the very improvement that competition is meant to encourage.

Schools should therefore combine competitive events with shared work. Teams can practise together, recognise personal improvement and discuss mistakes after results are announced. Clear rules and accessible opportunities matter more than elaborate trophies. Teachers should praise preparation, honesty and generous conduct as visibly as they praise victory.

The strongest objection is that such qualifications soften competition. I think they make it educational. A school is responsible for the development of all its pupils, including those outside the prize photograph. Competition brings out the best when winning remains one result of worthwhile effort, not the only proof that the effort mattered. Used with fairness and reflection, it can challenge students without teaching them to measure every person against a rank.

Option 5 [20]
Describe a situation in which listening carefully changed your first impression. Explain what you learned without turning the composition into a list of advice. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

I decided that our new neighbour was unfriendly before I had heard her speak more than three words. Each afternoon she passed our doorway carrying a canvas bag. She returned my greeting briefly and never stopped to talk. When our residents’ group asked for help with a reading event, her name was absent from the volunteers’ list.

I complained about this to my mother while arranging donated books. She suggested that I take a poster upstairs rather than invent an explanation for someone I hardly knew. I went reluctantly, already preparing a polite speech for another refusal.

The neighbour opened the door with a finger against her lips. From the next room came a faint call, and she asked whether I could wait. I noticed a medicine chart beside the clock and a chair positioned close to the bedroom doorway. When she returned, she apologised and explained that her father needed almost constant care.

This time I listened. Her short afternoon walks were errands fitted between his meals and medical appointments. She had seen our notice but could not promise hours away from home. She asked whether there was work she could do upstairs while remaining nearby.

I took her a box of books with damaged labels. Over the next week she repaired them, made a clear catalogue and suggested a simple borrowing register. She had worked in a library for years. On the day of the event, the neatly organised collection was the first thing our teacher praised.

I felt pleased, then ashamed of the pleasure. I had almost excluded the most experienced person available because I treated a brief conversation as a complete account of her life.

After the event I returned to thank her. We talked quietly while she checked the repaired books. Nothing about her circumstances had become easy, and she still could not stop at every doorway. What had changed was my willingness to ask, wait and hear the answer before deciding what another person’s behaviour meant.

The library’s oldest maps lived in a locked cabinet. Their brown edges and unfamiliar place names made them look important, but nobody seemed to know what to do with them. When Tara asked to borrow one for a geography project, the librarian was reluctant. She had spent years protecting the collection from damp, dust and careless fingers. A schoolbag seemed to her a more immediate danger than any of these. Tara did not argue that the maps belonged to everybody. Instead, she proposed a supervised afternoon at the long library table. She would use clean paper beneath each sheet, make notes in pencil and return every map before leaving. The librarian agreed to a temporary arrangement, watching from her desk as six pupils compared a map printed fifty years earlier with a recent one. At first they copied obvious changes: a housing estate where woodland had stood, a broad road replacing a narrow lane, a stream now marked as a drain. Then a question interrupted their neat columns. Why had the older map shown a footpath ending beside an apparently empty field? A retired railway worker who visited the library supplied an answer. The field had once hosted a weekly market; people had walked there from the station. The path had survived on paper after the market itself disappeared. The pupils began interviewing residents. Their work became meticulous: a memory was labelled as a memory, a date checked against records, an uncertainty left visible. Different accounts sometimes disagreed. Rather than choose the most colourful version, they recorded both and looked for another source. Their display eventually included two maps, short interviews and questions they had not yet settled. On the final afternoon the librarian stood before the display for a long time. The maps were neither damaged nor perfectly explained. They had become useful, and their unanswered details had made people look more carefully. She kept the cabinet locked, but added a notice inviting supervised use. Protection, she decided, need not mean permanent silence.
