Option 1 [20]
An ordinary journey became an experience you still remember. Describe the incident, your response and how it affected your judgement of another person. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

The bus stopped just beyond the bridge, and the driver switched off the engine. I looked at my watch. There were forty minutes until my music examination, and the road ahead had disappeared beneath a line of stationary vehicles.

An elderly passenger in the seat opposite began asking questions. How far was the examination centre? Did I have someone to call? Had I eaten? I answered impatiently. His calmness seemed almost insulting when everything important in my day was going wrong.

The driver returned from the front of the queue. A fallen branch had blocked the road. Traffic might move in half an hour, or it might not. I telephoned the centre, but nobody answered. The elderly man opened a small paper map that I had dismissed earlier as an unnecessary possession.

He pointed to a footbridge and a lane through the old market. “It is a walk,” he said, “but it reaches the same main road.” I hesitated. My instrument case was heavy, and I did not know the neighbourhood. He asked the conductor to confirm the route, then offered to walk as far as the market with me.

We moved slowly at first. He stopped at a shop to check the next turn rather than pretend certainty. At the square, a cycle-rickshaw driver agreed to take me the remaining distance. I climbed in, suddenly aware that the man had abandoned his own comfortable seat and added an inconvenient walk to his morning.

I reached the centre with four minutes to spare. My first scale shook, but the rest of the examination passed without disaster.

On the journey home I remembered how sharply I had answered his questions. I had mistaken practical concern for interference and composure for indifference. The most useful person on the bus had been the one I was least willing to listen to. I never learned his name, but I have remembered his folded map whenever another person’s quietness has tempted me to make a quick judgement.

Option 2 [20]
Describe a familiar market just before a festival. Use selected sights, sounds and interactions to convey its atmosphere rather than merely listing shops. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

By late afternoon the market seemed to be holding its breath before the festival. Strings of unlit bulbs crossed the narrow lane, and ladders leaned against shutters that would not close until midnight. Even the familiar vegetable stalls had surrendered a corner to flowers.

At the entrance, a woman separated marigolds with quick, practised movements. Orange petals clung to her sleeves. Beside her, a child counted completed garlands and carefully moved one from a heap whenever she lost her place. Customers bent close to inspect the flowers, then straightened into the stream of people before the next bargain began.

The sweet shop was warm enough to cloud its glass display. Inside, trays arrived from the kitchen with tiny metallic sounds as they touched the counter. The smell of hot sugar drifted into the lane and mixed with incense from a neighbouring stall. A boy holding an empty box watched the weighing scales more attentively than any adult around him.

Near the cloth shop, two customers argued over colours that looked almost identical to me. The shopkeeper carried each length to the doorway so that evening light could decide the matter. His assistant climbed a stool, took down another bundle and waited with the patience of someone who had repeated the same movement all day.

The lane narrowed where a delivery cart had stopped. People turned sideways, warning one another about the wheel. An impatient horn sounded from outside the market, but here a raised hand and a brief apology moved traffic more effectively than noise.

As darkness gathered, the first string of bulbs came on. The flowers deepened in colour, the glass counters brightened, and the exhausted faces behind them became easier to see. I had entered looking for decorations. I left noticing the work that made the celebration possible: hands tying, weighing, carrying and counting long after the purchases had become presents in someone else’s house. The market’s excitement was real, but it rested on an immense and mostly unnoticed effort.

Option 3 [20]
Describe the moment before, during and immediately after an important school performance, using a consistent viewpoint. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

Behind the curtain, the hall sounded much larger than it had during rehearsal. Chairs scraped, someone tested the microphone, and a brief squeal rose from the speakers. I held the first page of my script even though I had promised myself I would leave it in the dressing room.

Our stage manager raised one finger. A strip of light appeared beneath the curtain. Farah, standing beside me, silently moved her lips through the opening exchange. I wanted to reassure her, but my own mouth had become unhelpfully dry. From the other side came the principal’s final sentence and then applause.

The curtain opened before I felt ready. Beyond the bright lamps, the audience was a dark, uneven shape. I found the chair that marked my starting position and began. My first line sounded too quick. Then Farah answered, exactly as she had in rehearsal, and the scene acquired a rhythm that was easier to trust than my nerves.

Halfway through, a cup rolled off the table. It struck the floor and continued towards the front of the stage. For one terrible second I watched it instead of listening. Farah bent to retrieve it while keeping her character’s irritated expression. A few people laughed. The small accident had become part of the scene.

After that I could hear the audience reacting. Their laughter arrived in places I had expected silence, and their silence made the serious moments feel worth waiting for. The performance was no longer a sequence I had to survive. It was a conversation whose other half sat beyond the lights.

When the curtain finally closed, we remained still until the applause grew unmistakable. Someone behind me whispered, “We did it,” and the tension broke into hurried embraces and relieved laughter.

The hall was still full, but it no longer sounded threatening. I picked up my abandoned script and noticed the damp fold where my hand had held it. The paper recorded a fear that the performance itself had gradually made unnecessary.

Option 4 [20]
“Every school should reserve one afternoon a week for practical community work.” Give your views, supported by reasons and examples. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

Every school should reserve regular time for practical community work, provided that the work answers a real need and is organised responsibly. An afternoon each week can be valuable if learning and service are planned together rather than treated as a public display.

Classroom knowledge becomes more exact when students must use it. A group examining water waste has to measure quantities, compare records and explain a proposed repair. Pupils helping at a library must listen to readers and organise information clearly. These experiences connect subjects that can otherwise seem like separate collections of examination answers.

Community work can also correct easy assumptions. Students may discover that a problem already has local experts, that an apparently simple solution is unaffordable, or that the people receiving help want something different from what volunteers expected. Respectful listening is therefore part of the work, not a delay before it begins.

There are legitimate objections. Travel takes time, safety arrangements cost money, and badly managed projects can interrupt lessons while providing little benefit. Schools with fewer resources should not be expected to organise an impressive weekly expedition. Nor should students replace paid workers or undertake tasks for which they are untrained.

The answer is sensible scale. Projects can begin on the campus or in nearby institutions. A school might maintain an accessible reading collection, support a supervised recycling system or help document local history with permission. The partner organisation should help decide the task, and teachers should review progress rather than count photographs. A short written reflection can connect experience to evidence and identify what needs changing.

Examination preparation remains important, but it is not the whole purpose of education. An afternoon of carefully chosen work can develop judgement, cooperation and responsibility alongside academic skills. The proposal deserves support when service is sustained, useful and accountable. Its success should be measured by what improves for the community and what students learn to do thoughtfully, rather than by how busy or charitable the school appears.

Option 5 [20]
A school library closes temporarily and pupils must find another way to keep its activities going. Write a story or description centred on the situation. No picture is required. Write 300–350 words.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model composition

The notice on the library door gave us two weeks to remove everything from the lower shelves. A leaking pipe had damaged the wall, and repairs would close the room for a month. For our reading club, the timing seemed disastrous: the younger pupils’ story sessions were due to begin the following Monday.

At our first meeting, we spent twenty minutes explaining why the sessions would have to stop. Then our librarian asked a different question. “Which part of a library must stay inside this room?”

We looked at the shelves, the desks and the large issue register. The books needed protection, she explained, and borrowing needed a reliable record. Neither requirement demanded the same room every day. We began drawing up a smaller plan instead of waiting for the original one to become possible.

Each class agreed to hold one labelled box. Two sturdy tables in the covered corridor became an afternoon reading corner. We selected books carefully, keeping valuable or fragile volumes in secure storage. The principal approved the arrangement after checking that it left the passage clear.

Our first mobile session was untidy. A box arrived late, two pupils wanted the same book, and the wind scattered our loose paper slips. By the next day we had a clipboard, a borrowing timetable and a clear responsibility for returning every box. The younger children seemed more interested in the new arrangement than disappointed by the missing room.

When the library reopened, we were ready to carry everything back. Several pupils asked whether one corridor session could continue. Children who had never entered the library had begun borrowing because they encountered books on their normal route through school.

The repaired room remained important, but the interruption had changed our idea of its purpose. We had thought that keeping the library open meant keeping its door unlocked. Now we understood that access also depended on bringing books, records and willing readers into contact, wherever a safe and useful meeting place could be made.

Option 2 [10]
Write to a friend describing an unexpected responsibility you handled during a school event. Explain what happened, how you managed it and what you would do differently.
Worked solution / model answer
One complete model letter

12 Lake Road
Rivapur
10 January 2027

Dear Meera,

At our school exhibition yesterday, I unexpectedly had to supervise the entrance desk when a volunteer became unwell. I had prepared only for my own science demonstration, so the sudden change made me nervous.

I first asked the teacher what decisions I could make. Then I separated visitors with bookings from those who needed information and asked a classmate to guide families to the hall. When a younger pupil could not find his group, I kept him at the desk and contacted the supervising teacher instead of letting him search alone.

Everything eventually ran smoothly, but I learned that enthusiasm cannot replace clear instructions. Next time I would ask for an emergency contact list and a written allocation of duties before the event begins.

Yours affectionately,
Riya

For eleven years the afternoon bus had stopped beside a banyan tree on the edge of the village. There was no signboard. A stone bench, a tea stall and the passengers’ habit were enough to identify the place. When roadworks began, the transport office announced that the stop would move half a kilometre uphill for a month. Most of the regular passengers accepted the change without complaint. On the first afternoon at the new stop, Arun noticed that Mrs D’Souza was missing. She usually boarded with a cloth bag of vegetables and asked him whether his examinations had begun. At home he mentioned her absence. His father pointed out that an uphill walk which took a student five minutes might be difficult for somebody using a walking stick. Arun had thought of the change as a distance, not as an effort. The next day he walked the route before school. The temporary stop stood beside a busy bend, with no bench and little space away from traffic. He took photographs, but his teacher advised him that a persuasive complaint needed more than alarming pictures. He counted passengers, spoke to the driver and asked the road supervisor where a safe stopping place might be possible. The supervisor explained that the old location could not remain open while a trench crossed its approach. Arun’s first suggestion, to stop directly outside Mrs D’Souza’s house, was rejected. The road there was too narrow for a bus to wait safely. A second suggestion was more practical: the level entrance of the community hall, reached without crossing the works, had room for passengers to stand clear of vehicles. The hall committee agreed to provide two chairs during bus hours. The transport office approved the proposal after a trial. Nobody described it as a victory. The driver still had to adjust his timing and passengers still walked farther than before. But Mrs D’Souza returned to the bus. Arun discovered that a useful solution could be modest, inconvenient and better than the alternatives at the same time.
