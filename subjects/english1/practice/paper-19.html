4 [20]
The seed exchange began with a table outside the community hall and a hand-painted request: bring what you can spare, take only what you can grow. The organisers expected a pleasant morning of trading. Instead, the first disagreement began before the first packet changed hands. Two gardeners used the same local name for different plants. One of them insisted that the label was wrong. The other unfolded a paper containing a description in her mother’s handwriting. The argument might have ended with somebody leaving if a volunteer had not suggested keeping both names and adding a description. The packet acquired an unusually long label, including the shape of the fruit and the village from which the seed had come. Other problems followed. Some seeds had been saved after cross-pollination; their next crop might not resemble the parent exactly. Others had been stored in a damp cupboard. An experienced grower explained that certainty could not be added to a packet by writing confidently on it. The organisers made room for notes about storage, age and uncertainty, and stopped calling every locally saved seed an unchanged old variety. By noon the exchange looked less tidy than planned. A temporary notice invited recipients to report what grew, and pupils offered to collect those accounts at the next meeting. A gardener who had initially brought nothing took a small packet after describing the sunny patch beside her kitchen. Another returned a packet because she realised that its plant would need more space than she had. The exchange did not guarantee a harvest. Some seeds would fail, and some labels would be corrected. Its success was a new habit of sharing useful limits along with useful material. The longest label remained on the table until the end. When its owner finally took it home, she copied both names rather than choosing a winner.
