The ferry office’s ledger recorded numbers, not stories: passengers, fares, fuel and the time of each crossing. When a temporary reduction in service was proposed, a district officer used the figures to identify the least crowded journey. Cancelling that crossing appeared a reasonable way to save money without seriously affecting the route. The ferryman, Joseph, asked to keep an additional record for a fortnight. He did not dispute the passenger count. The small early crossing often carried fewer than ten people, while later ones were full. But he knew that the people on the quiet journey rarely had the freedom to take another. Some worked at the hospital, and two travelled onward by a connecting bus which left soon after the ferry arrived. Joseph’s new record contained occupations and onward connections, collected only with passengers’ consent. He did not write private details. He also recorded tides, delays and the number of people who returned without boarding when a crossing was late. The figures revealed an awkward fact: the apparently least useful journey supported activities whose value was not represented by its fares. The officer visited the landing. She asked whether the ferry could carry goods on the early journey to improve its income. Local traders agreed to a trial with small deliveries that did not delay passengers. The timetable remained, although another quiet crossing was combined with a nearby service. Nobody received everything requested. Joseph continued the ledger. He did not believe that numbers had become less important. They had become more informative because someone had asked what they omitted. When the trial was reviewed, the officer thanked him for the extra columns. He pointed to the original ones. Without those records, he said, they would not have known where a question needed to be asked.
