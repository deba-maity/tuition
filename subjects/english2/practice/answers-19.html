Section B — Drama

Answer at least ONE question from this section. Across Sections B–D, answer FOUR questions in total. Each is 16 marks.

2 [16]
Julius Caesar Act III Context summary: Caesar is killed at the Capitol; Brutus permits Antony to address the citizens under conditions.
(1) [3]
Explain why Brutus refuses to allow the conspirators to kill Antony as well. What does this reveal about his political judgement?
Worked solution / model answer
Brutus wants an act against tyranny rather than indiscriminate slaughter; he underestimates Antony’s independent power and assumes that Caesar’s death removes the danger. His ethical intentions accompany a serious strategic error.
(2) [3]
Why does Brutus use the contrast between his love for Caesar and his love for Rome?
Worked solution / model answer
He presents assassination as public duty overcoming personal affection. The contrast asks citizens to judge him as a patriot. His appeal depends on their accepting that Caesar threatened their freedom.
(3) [3]
State three conditions Brutus imposes on Antony’s funeral speech.
Worked solution / model answer
Antony must not blame the conspirators; he must acknowledge their permission; he must speak after Brutus at the same public place. Credit equivalent precise accounts of the conditions.
(4) [3]
Why does Caesar reject Metellus Cimber’s petition, and what does his response reveal about his self-image?
Worked solution / model answer
Metellus seeks the recall of his banished brother. Caesar refuses to be moved by pleading and presents his judgement as fixed. This confidence in constancy makes his vulnerability to the conspirators ironic.
(5) [4]
Compare Brutus’s and Antony’s methods of addressing the citizens.
Worked solution / model answer
Brutus offers a concise justification based on honour, reason and civic freedom. Antony uses irony, concrete memories, wounds, the will and controlled emotion. Both seek public judgement, but Antony adapts his speech to the crowd’s developing responses.
3 [16]
Julius Caesar Act V Context summary: The armies meet at Philippi; mistaken reports lead to Cassius’s death, followed later by Brutus’s defeat.
(1) [3]
Why does Cassius send Titinius towards the approaching troops?
Worked solution / model answer
Cassius needs to know whether the troops are friends or enemies. He sends Titinius to investigate and relies on Pindarus’s distant observations instead of obtaining a reliable direct report.
(2) [3]
How does Strato assist Brutus at the end?
Worked solution / model answer
After other companions refuse to kill him, Brutus asks Strato to hold the sword and runs upon it. This preserves Brutus’s sense of choosing his own end after military defeat.
(3) [3]
What disagreement occurs between Antony and Octavius about their positions in battle?
Worked solution / model answer
Antony directs Octavius to the left; Octavius chooses the right. Octavius’s refusal to follow the instruction reveals independence within their alliance rather than complete submission to Antony.
(4) [3]
Why is Cassius disturbed by the birds accompanying the army?
Worked solution / model answer
He contrasts the earlier eagles with ravens, crows and kites and interprets the change as an ominous sign. His admission that he now partly credits omens shows anxiety before battle.
(5) [4]
Explain Antony’s final judgement of Brutus and its qualification of the play’s political conflict.
Worked solution / model answer
Antony distinguishes Brutus’s concern for the public good from the envy motivating other conspirators. He praises Brutus’s balanced nature and honour, acknowledging moral worth in a defeated opponent while not undoing the disastrous consequences of his act.

Section C — Prose

Answer at least ONE question from this section. Across Sections B–D, answer FOUR questions in total. Each is 16 marks.

4 [16]
The Pedestrian Context summary: Leonard Mead walks through a screen-dominated city and is questioned by an automated police car.
(1) [3]
Why is walking simply to observe considered abnormal in this society?
Worked solution / model answer
Leisure is expected to occur indoors with screens. Mead’s independent curiosity and unstructured movement do not fit the system’s prescribed behaviour.
(2) [3]
How do descriptions of the houses contribute to the atmosphere?
Worked solution / model answer
The houses seem dark, silent and tomb-like apart from screen light. The imagery suggests isolation and diminished public life rather than a lively neighbourhood.
(3) [3]
What is ironic about the institution to which Mead is taken?
Worked solution / model answer
Ordinary independent behaviour is classified as a condition requiring correction. The society’s idea of normality is itself disturbing to the reader.
(4) [3]
Why does the contrast between Mead’s home and the others matter near the end?
Worked solution / model answer
His brightly lit home contrasts with the surrounding darkness and symbolises a more active individual life. He is removed from that life by the conformity-enforcing system.
(5) [4]
Explain how setting and the encounter develop the conflict between individuality and conformity.
Worked solution / model answer
Mead’s attentive walking contrasts with the empty screen-bound city. The police questions deny the legitimacy of harmless difference. His detention turns social sameness into coercion rather than a free preference.
5 [16]
With the Photographer Context summary: A customer asks for a photograph; the photographer treats his real features as defects to be altered.
(1) [3]
Explain the irony in the photographer’s confidence about retouching.
Worked solution / model answer
He regards increasingly drastic alterations as achievements. For the narrator, each improvement makes the picture less useful as a likeness. Technical success becomes failure in the customer’s terms.
(2) [3]
Why is the narrator’s purpose in visiting the studio important to the conflict?
Worked solution / model answer
He wants a recognisable likeness of himself. The photographer assumes that technically improving the face is more important. Their incompatible definitions of a satisfactory photograph create the conflict.
(3) [3]
How does first-person narration contribute to the humour?
Worked solution / model answer
Readers follow the customer’s increasing discomfort and indignation directly. His restrained account of absurd professional behaviour exposes the photographer’s self-importance.
(4) [3]
How does the narrator’s reaction at the end expose the limits of professional expertise?
Worked solution / model answer
He rejects the altered portrait because it does not represent him. The expert’s ability to manipulate an image does not authorise him to replace the subject’s wishes.
(5) [4]
Discuss the conflict between appearance and identity using the visit, the alterations and the ending.
Worked solution / model answer
The visit begins with a wish for a personal likeness. Repeated criticism and retouching replace the individual with a supposed ideal. The narrator’s rejection asserts that identity and recognisability matter more than imposed perfection.

Section D — Poetry

Answer at least ONE question from this section. Across Sections B–D, answer FOUR questions in total. Each is 16 marks.

6 [16]
A Considerable Speck Context summary: A writer notices a minute moving creature on his manuscript and observes it before deciding its fate.
(1) [3]
How does humour affect the speaker’s reflection?
Worked solution / model answer
The disproportion between a writer and a tiny visitor makes the meditation playful. It avoids pompous moralising while still taking perceived intelligence seriously.
(2) [3]
Which aspect of the speck’s behaviour suggests intelligence to the speaker?
Worked solution / model answer
Its apparently purposeful movement, hesitation and response to danger seem different from mechanical motion. The inference comes from observed behaviour rather than size.
(3) [3]
What does the decision to spare the speck reveal?
Worked solution / model answer
It reveals respect for a perceived independent mind and the deliberate restraint of power. The speaker values mental life without requiring physical grandeur.
(4) [3]
Why is the title an apparent contradiction?
Worked solution / model answer
A speck is minute, whereas considerable suggests importance. The poem separates physical smallness from the significance of thought or life.
(5) [4]
Explain how observation changes the speaker’s response and supports the title.
Worked solution / model answer
What initially seems negligible displays purposeful behaviour. The speaker revises his judgement and chooses to spare it. The creature becomes considerable through the intelligence he perceives, giving the title its comic and moral force.
7 [16]
Haunted Houses Context summary: The speaker imagines past inhabitants continuing to share the places occupied by living people.
(1) [3]
How does the poem connect the material and spiritual worlds?
Worked solution / model answer
It presents them as overlapping rather than completely separate. Images of passage and connection allow the unseen to influence ordinary experience.
(2) [3]
How does the speaker question exclusive ownership of a house?
Worked solution / model answer
The living possess legal title, but the lives and memories of earlier occupants also belong to the place. Ownership is set against historical and emotional continuity.
(3) [3]
What does the title suggest once the whole poem is considered?
Worked solution / model answer
Being haunted means retaining traces of earlier lives. The title broadens from a ghost-story expectation into a meditation on memory and spiritual presence.
(4) [3]
What effect does the image of invisible guests produce?
Worked solution / model answer
It suggests that the present contains more lives and memories than the eye perceives. Ordinary social activity becomes a meeting of visible and invisible company.
(5) [4]
Explain how two images develop the continuing relationship between past and present.
Worked solution / model answer
The quiet movement of former inhabitants through familiar rooms makes the past part of ordinary domestic life. These presences do not attack the living; they suggest that a place retains the lives once lived there. The image of unseen guests extends the same idea into a social gathering: visible people are not its only company, because memory and earlier lives also occupy the space. Both images make past and present coexist. The calm, reflective tone turns haunting into continuity rather than a conventional supernatural threat.
