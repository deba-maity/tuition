Section B — Drama

Answer at least ONE question from this section. Across Sections B–D, answer FOUR questions in total. Each is 16 marks.

2 [16]
Julius Caesar Act IV Context summary: The triumvirs prepare their campaign. Brutus and Cassius quarrel, reconcile, and discuss marching to Philippi.
(1) [3]
How is the quarrel between Brutus and Cassius resolved?
Worked solution / model answer
Cassius offers Brutus his dagger and exposes his hurt; Brutus softens, acknowledges anger and accepts reconciliation. Their personal affection temporarily overcomes accusations and political differences.
(2) [3]
Explain the disagreement over money sent to Brutus’s army.
Worked solution / model answer
Brutus says Cassius denied funds when Brutus would not raise them dishonourably. Cassius denies deliberately refusing him and blames the messenger. Distrust turns a logistical dispute into a personal quarrel.
(3) [3]
What does Brutus disclose about Portia, and why is the disclosure significant here?
Worked solution / model answer
Portia has died by swallowing fire, distressed by Brutus’s absence and the political situation. Brutus’s controlled disclosure explains part of his emotional strain and shows the personal cost of the conflict.
(4) [3]
Give Cassius’s reason for waiting for the enemy and Brutus’s reason for marching to Philippi.
Worked solution / model answer
Cassius wants the enemy to tire and consume resources while their army rests. Brutus fears the enemy will gather support from the people between them and argues that they should act at a favourable moment.
(5) [4]
How does Caesar’s ghost affect the close of Act IV?
Worked solution / model answer
The apparition identifies itself as Brutus’s evil spirit and promises another meeting at Philippi. Brutus questions it but cannot control it. It connects Caesar’s continuing influence with the approaching battle and Brutus’s fate.
3 [16]
Julius Caesar Act III Context summary: Caesar is killed at the Capitol; Brutus permits Antony to address the citizens under conditions.
(1) [3]
Why does Caesar reject Metellus Cimber’s petition, and what does his response reveal about his self-image?
Worked solution / model answer
Metellus seeks the recall of his banished brother. Caesar refuses to be moved by pleading and presents his judgement as fixed. This confidence in constancy makes his vulnerability to the conspirators ironic.
(2) [3]
Why does Antony delay reading Caesar’s will?
Worked solution / model answer
Delay arouses curiosity and makes the crowd demand it. The prospect of benefits establishes a personal connection with Caesar. Antony directs the crowd while presenting himself as reluctant to inflame it.
(3) [3]
How does Antony use Caesar’s refusal of the crown to challenge Brutus’s argument?
Worked solution / model answer
Brutus claims dangerous ambition. Antony recalls Caesar refusing the crown three times, supplying a public action apparently inconsistent with that charge. It helps move the crowd from assent to doubt.
(4) [3]
Explain how Antony’s repeated praise of the conspirators changes its effect during the funeral speech.
Worked solution / model answer
The apparently respectful description becomes ironic as Antony supplies evidence against the ambition charge. Repetition lets listeners reach the accusation themselves while Antony appears to obey the restriction on blaming the conspirators.
(5) [4]
Compare Brutus’s and Antony’s methods of addressing the citizens.
Worked solution / model answer
Brutus offers a concise justification based on honour, reason and civic freedom. Antony uses irony, concrete memories, wounds, the will and controlled emotion. Both seek public judgement, but Antony adapts his speech to the crowd’s developing responses.

Answer at least ONE question from this section. Across Sections B–D, answer FOUR questions in total. Each is 16 marks.

4 [16]
The Last Lesson Context summary: Franz arrives at school to learn that an official order has ended French teaching in Alsace and Lorraine.
(1) [3]
What does Franz discover about the official order?
Worked solution / model answer
Only German is to be taught in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine. The lesson is therefore the last French lesson under M. Hamel.
(2) [3]
How does Franz’s attitude towards his books change?
Worked solution / model answer
Formerly burdensome books become valued companions when he realises he may lose access to their language. Imminent loss makes him recognise neglected educational opportunities.
(3) [3]
Why is M. Hamel unable to complete his final spoken farewell?
Worked solution / model answer
Emotion overwhelms him. He turns to the blackboard and writes a patriotic declaration, making the silent action carry what he cannot say aloud.
(4) [3]
What unusual features of the classroom signal a change?
Worked solution / model answer
The usual noise is absent, M. Hamel wears formal clothes and village elders sit at the back. These details prepare Franz for the seriousness of the final lesson.
(5) [4]
Trace Franz’s change and connect it to language and identity.
Worked solution / model answer
Franz begins afraid of a school exercise and willing to avoid it. The order makes him understand language as a threatened shared inheritance. Attentive listening and regret replace indifference; the teacher’s final act gives that recognition public emotional force.
5 [16]
The Elevator Context summary: Martin fears the small elevator in his building; encounters with a large woman intensify his anxiety.
(1) [3]
Give three features of the elevator that contribute to Martin’s fear.
Worked solution / model answer
Its confined space, poor lighting and unreliable-seeming movement/closing mechanism make it threatening to him. Explain how each feature increases claustrophobia rather than merely listing adjectives.
(2) [3]
Why is the woman’s physical presence particularly disturbing inside the elevator?
Worked solution / model answer
She occupies much of the small space and seems to stare at Martin. Her proximity combines his existing fear of confinement with uncertainty about her intentions.
(3) [3]
Why is Martin’s injury important to the development of suspense?
Worked solution / model answer
It limits his freedom of movement and makes escape or choosing the stairs harder. The physical injury turns emotional fear into greater practical helplessness.
(4) [3]
How does Martin’s father respond to his fear?
Worked solution / model answer
He dismisses it as weakness and expects him to overcome it. The response makes Martin ashamed and leaves him unable to seek reassuring understanding.
(5) [4]
How do setting, parental response and the open ending work together?
Worked solution / model answer
The confined elevator creates fear; the father’s dismissal isolates Martin; injury restricts his choices. The unresolved final encounter leaves readers sharing uncertainty instead of receiving a complete explanation.

Section D — Poetry

Answer at least ONE question from this section. Across Sections B–D, answer FOUR questions in total. Each is 16 marks.

6 [16]
The Power of Music Context summary: Bhishma Lochan Sharma sings with such exaggerated effects that people and animals suffer until a goat intervenes.
(1) [3]
Explain the role of sound and rhythm in the comic narrative.
Worked solution / model answer
The lively rhythm carries the performance forward with a force out of proportion to its musical worth. Repeated distress among listeners makes the song sound relentless rather than pleasurable. Their appeals to stop contrast with the singer’s persistence, so each continuing disturbance increases the comic tension. The goat’s abrupt physical intervention supplies a decisive change: action silences a voice that ordinary requests could not restrain. Sound, narrative pace and the sudden ending together expose the singer’s self-importance.
(2) [3]
How does the poem treat appeals to stop the singing?
Worked solution / model answer
Ordinary responses fail to restrain the performer. Repetition of distress and his persistence prepare for an unexpected physical interruption.
(3) [3]
What is ironic about the title?
Worked solution / model answer
Music conventionally suggests pleasure or harmony. Here its power is represented as disruptive and painful, making the title praise the performance in a comic opposite sense.
(4) [3]
How do exaggerated geographical effects create humour?
Worked solution / model answer
The voice appears to disturb an impossibly wide area. Hyperbole magnifies an unpleasant performance into a fantastic public calamity.
(5) [4]
How do hyperbole, contrast and the final reversal work together?
Worked solution / model answer
Impossible effects inflate the song into a catastrophe; the singer’s delight contrasts with widespread distress. A goat’s simple action defeats the supposedly powerful performer, completing the satire of self-important bad singing.
7 [16]
The Glove and the Lions Context summary: At a royal lion-fight a lady drops her glove among the animals to test her lover publicly.
(1) [3]
How does the final action change the meaning of the heroic rescue?
Worked solution / model answer
The danger is overcome, but the emotional reward is refused. The act affirms self-respect rather than the lady’s power to demand sacrifice.
(2) [3]
Why is the setting at a royal court important?
Worked solution / model answer
The spectators and king make the act a public display. The lady’s demand is shaped by the possibility of gaining attention and admiration before an audience.
(3) [3]
What does the lady expect her lover to prove?
Worked solution / model answer
She expects him to retrieve the glove as evidence of devotion and to make her the admired centre of a spectacular romantic gesture.
(4) [3]
Explain the irony of the lady’s plan.
Worked solution / model answer
She seeks a public proof of love that will enhance her prestige. The successful retrieval instead becomes the occasion for public rejection.
(5) [4]
Discuss the distinction between love and vanity in the lady’s test and its outcome.
Worked solution / model answer
The test values spectacle over the beloved’s safety. The lover shows courage but refuses that condition of affection. The king’s approval makes the closing judgement favour dignity and sincere care over theatrical display.
